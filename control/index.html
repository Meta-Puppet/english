How a Pinch Moves the Other Arm

MetaPuppet converts the pinch motion of a participant's own fingers into a force that lifts the participant's other forearm. A record of the hardware and control implementation that connects the input to output using one UDP and one cable.

Prologue Overview

Preface

The core of MetaPuppet's experience is not just software. If camera images and VR drawings are records of transmission and synthesis, this record goes beyond that and is about the part that actually exerts force on the participants' bodies. Participants move their fingers. The hand tracking on the VR side detects the finger movements and sends one finger to the Raspberry Pi via UDP. Pi sets the value as the target position and uses PD control to follow the servo, and the rotation of the servo pulls the cable wrapped around the drum, which lifts the participant's other forearm. The person who moves his or her finger becomes the one being moved by an external force in the next moment[1].

The recording traces the force path from the participant's fingertips to their forearm, following the actual moving cord. Hand tracking and UDP transmission on the input side are implemented in Unity (C#), and reception, PD control, and servo drive are implemented in Python on Raspberry Pi.

A view of the frame of the actual machine from the front. Cords extend from the two servos on the crossbar, each connected to a wrist cuff. The laptop in front is for opening the Motor Pi dashboard and calibrating it.
  1. The idea that the two roles of “the self who moves” and “the self who is moved” occur simultaneously within the same body is the very framework for research on the sense of body ownership and agency using this experience.
Chapter 01 Input / Hand Tracking

Detecting a Pinch in VR

Input is handled by a component on the Unity side called MetaPuppetPinchUdpSender. First, obtain the positions of the thumb tip (ThumbTip) and index finger tip (IndexTip) from the XR Hands joint, and calculate each frame with the midpoint between the two points as the "pinch point" and the distance between the two points as the "pinch distance." When the pinch distance becomes less than a predetermined threshold (default 2.5cm), it is determined that the pinch is in progress. If the headset's hand tracking loses a joint, there is also a fallback path that uses the pinch strength of Meta's pointing input (MetaAim), so that the input itself does not disappear immediately even if joint tracking is interrupted.

While the pinch continues, calculate how far the pinch point has moved in the vertical direction from the previous frame (verticalDeltaCm) and the cumulative amount of movement since the pinch started (verticalTotalCm) for each frame in cm. The movement of lifting and lowering the forearm uses only this vertical component. Even if the pinch detection method (joint tracking or MetaAim) is switched midway through, the accumulated verticalTotalCm itself is carried over and only the origin is replaced (Rebase) in order to prevent the motor target value from jumping at the moment of switching.

Chapter 02 UDP Packet

The UDP Packet from Unity to the Raspberry Pi

The calculated value is packed into a unique 36-byte packet and sent via UDP. The reason why we chose UDP over TCP is that, similar to video transmission (the unique UDP protocol discussed in What we worked hard to implement in the communication infrastructure), even if some packets are missing, it is more important in applications that generate force to continue sending the “current finger position” than to send old values ​​later.

Packet layout of MetaPuppetPinchUdpSender.cs (version 2, 36 bytes)
char[4] Magic; // 'M','P','P','H'
byte Version; // = 2
byte Hand; // 0 = left, 1 = right
byte MotorId; // Servo ID driven by this packet
uint32 Sequence;
double TimestampSeconds;
byte IsPinch;
float VerticalDeltaCm; // Difference from previous frame
float VerticalTotalCm; // Accumulation since pinch start
float PinchDistanceCm;
float PinchStrength;

The reason why MotorId is included in the packet itself in version 2 is to allow you to explicitly specify which hand drives which servo in the Inspector on the Unity side (as Left Hand Motor Id / Right Hand Motor Id) instead of relying only on the fixed mapping on the Pi side. Version 1 packets (32-byte old format without the MotorId field) are also accepted as backward compatible, in which case the Pi startup arguments (--left-hand-servo-id / --right-hand-servo-id) will be the fallback mapping.

Chapter 03 Two-Pi Architecture

Why We Use a Camera Pi and a Motor Pi

MetaPuppet uses two Raspberry Pis. The Camera Pi captures, encodes, and transmits stereo video over a custom UDP protocol. The Motor Pi drives the servos, which are the focus of this article. A separate article covers the Camera Pi. Both camera capture and encoding and the servo's serial communication and PD control loop are latency-sensitive, but their workloads are very different. On one Pi, either workload could slow the other down.

The Camera Pi's integrated dashboard (mp_control.py, port 7000) directly proxies the Motor Pi's API to /api/motor/*, making it possible to check both the camera status and servo status from a single screen on the Camera Pi's dashboard in the field. The two Pis are connected by Tailscale[1], allowing fixed wiring (Ethernet) for production use and Tailscale for remote debugging to coexist as separate interfaces on the same Pi.

  1. Tailscale is a WireGuard-based mesh VPN. It is a service that allows devices on different networks to allocate fixed private IP addresses and communicate directly with each other, and is used as a route for developers to remotely check and operate the status of devices, separate from the venue's LAN.
Chapter 04 Serial Bus Servo

STS Servos and Custom Serial Packets

The drums are rotated by two Feetech STS series serial bus servos (ID 5 and 6). Unlike PWM servos for hobbyists, STS servos can be used to hang multiple units on a single half-duplex asynchronous serial line (default 1 Mbps), distinguishing them by ID, and read and write position, speed, and torque on/off information via registers. Of the scservo_sdk distributed for this series, Motor Pi only uses PortHandler, which opens and closes the serial port, and assembles the packets it actually sends on its own.

Packet format (STS bus protocol)
0xFF 0xFF ID Length Instruction [Params...] Checksum
// Lower 1 byte of Checksum = ~(ID + Length + Instruction + Params...)
// ID 0xFE is the broadcast ID. Used for simultaneous writing to multiple servos (Sync Write, 0x83)
Looking up at the top of the frame. Two STS servos are secured to 3D printed mounts, and the cord extends to the wrist cuff.

After sending a ping to each servo at startup and checking the response, write 1 to the mode switching register (address 33) to set it to "wheel mode" [1] and enable torque on (address 40). After that, the current position and current speed are read out in 4 bytes from address 56, and the target position (Sync Write, address 42) and rotation speed (Sync Write, address 41) are written together with the IDs and values ​​for the two machines in one broadcast packet. The reason for writing without waiting for a response (write_packet_no_response) is that position/velocity commands continue every frame, and if you wait for a response from the servo every time you write, the cycle of the PD control loop will be delayed accordingly.

  1. The STS servo can be switched between the normal “servo mode,” which rotates to a specified angle and stops, and the “wheel mode,” which allows it to continue rotating without any upper or lower limits. Since MetaPuppet needs to keep winding the cable around the drum, it uses a wheel mode with no limit to the angle and commands the rotation speed rather than the target position.
Chapter 05 PD Control Loop

Using PD Control to Track a Pinch-Driven Target

There are two main control modes for Motor Pi. Input from Unity is always received in the pd mode in the field, with a relative mode for manual jogging (moving the servo a certain amount on the spot) and a pd mode that continues to track the position of the finger that can be reached via UDP.

In PD mode, the target count value is the "cumulative movement amount since the start of pinch (total_cm)" contained in the packet multiplied by counts_per_cm (number of encoder counts per cm determined by calibration, default 290). The difference between this target value and the estimated position held internally is defined as an error, and a term proportional to the size of the error (Kp) and a term proportional to the rate of change of the error (Kd) are added together to create the rotational speed command value sent to the servo [1].

Key points of pi_motor_webui.py (PD tracking loop)
target = total_cm * counts_per_cm * direction
error = target - estimated_position
speed = clamp(Kp * error + Kd * (error - previous_error) / dt, -speed_limit, speed_limit)
// If the error is small and the speed is also small, set it to 0 (dead band)
// If it is not 0, raise the speed so that it does not fall below the minimum speed (min_command_speed) (measure against stiction)
// Clip the amount of change from the previous speed by pd_accel_limit * dt (suppress sudden acceleration)
estimated_position += speed * dt

What you need to be careful about here is that the "estimated position" used as the basis for error calculation is not the actual position read back from the servo, but an estimated value only inside the Pi, which is simply the time integration of the speed command that was sent until just before. In other words, this PD control is not a strict closed loop that feeds back the servo's actual position every cycle, but a semi-open loop follower that approaches the target while estimating its current position from the commanded speed. Since position reading of the serial bus servo cannot be performed as frequently as command transmission, this design is a result of prioritizing stability of the control cycle over accuracy. The deadband, minimum speed, and upper limit of acceleration serve as buffers in actual operation to prevent the servo from continuing to jerk back and forth before reaching the target position, and to prevent sudden shock from being transmitted to the participant's arm.

PD control is a control method that generates commands based on the difference between the target value and the current value (proportional term, P), as well as the momentum of change (differential term, D), which indicates whether the difference is narrowing or widening. If there is only a proportional term, it is easy to cause vibrations that go too far near the target and then return, and the differential term plays the role of putting a brake on that vibration.
Chapter 06 Web Dashboard / Calibration

On-Site Calibration and Manual Control

When Motor Pi starts up, it exposes Flask's Web Dashboard on port 8080. Here, there is a table that displays the current position, target position, speed, and torque status of each servo every 250ms, and a table that displays the current position, target position, speed, and torque status of each servo. There is a manual jog that allows you to directly move the servo in mm increments, a Set Initial button that reregisters the current position as the "initial position" on the spot, and an adjustment panel that allows you to rewrite the PD's Kp/Kd, counts/cm, acceleration upper limit, and minimum command speed on the spot without restarting the process.

Manual jog is a simple implementation different from PD mode, in which the specified number of mm is converted to counts_per_cm, and the servo is rotated at a speed calculated backwards from the minimum time (min-jog-ms, default 120ms), which is the expected number of milliseconds for the movement. This is primarily a function for manually adjusting the cable tension and drum winding position before participants put it on, and it shares the same counts_per_cm physical calibration values ​​used in PD mode.

Chapter 07 Safety

Safety Design for a Mechanism That Applies Force to the Body

Even if the camera or video pipeline breaks, the only thing that will happen is that the video will be distorted. However, because this mechanism generates the force that actually moves the participant's arm, it is not possible to design how it will break using software alone. In an implementation, multiple safety measures with different characteristics are piled up.

Do not run the motor immediately after startup

When Motor Pi starts up, it accepts drive commands via UDP, but the udp_enabled flag is turned off by default. The servo responds to commands only after the mechanism is visually checked from the dashboard and staff explicitly enable UDP.

A physical emergency stop button located at the scene. A last resort independent of the emergency stop on the software side (two systems on the Unity side)

Apart from the software design, there is also a physical emergency stop button on site. This is for directly intervening in the power supply system without going through the software on the Unity side or the Motor Pi side, and in the sense that it can stop the mechanism no matter what state the software is in, it is a last resort that is one step more independent than the two systems that will be explained later.

Unity also has two emergency stop systems that can be stopped independently by either participants or staff. One is for staff and is activated immediately by pressing the Escape key on the keyboard. The other one is for the person wearing it, and is activated by a gesture of holding both hands together for five seconds. This length of 5 seconds was designed to be both long enough so that it would not be activated accidentally even if both hands accidentally pinched at the same time during play, and short enough that the player could stop it on their own without waiting for the staff's reaction.

Regardless of which route is used, a stop packet is immediately sent to all MetaPuppetPinchUdpSender (and related wire drive components) in the scene, and from then on, only empty safety packets are sent instead of actual drive packets. This stopped state is latched for the life of the process and cannot be cleared simply by reloading the scene, and the warning text continues to be displayed in red in the HMD. The design was based on the judgment that it would be meaningless if the system could be inadvertently returned to its original state after it had been stopped for safety reasons.

The dead band and acceleration limit (Chapter 5) on the PD control side also work as part of this safety design. If the servo continues to vibrate slightly before the target value is reached, or if the target suddenly changes and a shock is transmitted to the arm, these are both failures that have a different gravity than a dropped frame in a video, since both are mechanisms that come into direct contact with the participant's body.

Epilogue Summary

Afterword

The amount of finger pinch is packed into a UDP packet, converted to servo rotation speed using PD control, and the forearm is lifted using a cable wrapped around a drum. This is the only route that can be described in text, but every section of the route has constraints calculated from the premise that force is directly applied to the participants' bodies. The starting point of the design is how to track the target value (the reason why it is PD control and not a simple position command), the driving start conditions (UDP is off by default), and how to stop (two independent emergency stops and latches).

The experimental framework for the question that this hardware is trying to answer - what is the effect of the experience of moving another part of your body using an external force when you initiate a movement with your own fingers? is summarized in a separate poster draft. This record is a simple record of wiring, packets, and gain that I wrote in order to actually make that question come true at the venue.
